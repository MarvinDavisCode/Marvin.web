Biography

How I got here, and what I'm working toward

I am a mental health and psychology professional working at the intersection of clinical psychology, mental health intervention, implementation science, neuroscience, and artificial intelligence. My work is organized around one question: how do we move from knowing what works in mental health to building systems that can deliver it effectively, ethically, and at scale?

My foundational knowledge in psychology was acquired at Egerton University, where I began to understand psychology as something that extends beyond the academic and clinical boundaries and into the institutions and communities around it. I served first as Secretary General (2022–2023) and later as Vice-Chairperson (2023–2024) of the Egerton University Psychology Students' Organization, roles that gave me early exposure to organizational leadership, representation, and the work of turning ideas into coordinated action.

My professional experience in mental health took shape through the Shamiri Institute, where I progressed from Fellow to working with the HQ team under Clinical Operations. Starting in direct delivery — facilitating individual and group sessions, monitoring participant progress, and escalating high-risk cases through appropriate referral pathways — I moved over time into the infrastructure behind delivery: coordinating projects and teams, managing deliverables, supporting planning and evaluation, and improving processes for the colleagues who followed. That progression changed the questions I was asking, from whether an intervention works to what allows it to keep working as it grows.

I have since seen that same question from other vantage points. Supporting the Nakuru County Department of Mental Health Technical Working Group as Assistant Project Lead showed me mental health from a public-sector and systems perspective — work plans, monitoring frameworks, and stakeholder coordination across county initiatives. At Kivuli Wellness Kenya, I worked closer to the community, coordinating outreach, training, and partnerships between schools, healthcare facilities, and local leaders. Together, these experiences gave me a view of mental health across several connected levels: the person receiving support, the practitioner delivering it, the programme coordinating it, and the system it depends on.

My work on mental health sits on layers of a multidisciplinary approach. Clinical psychology gives me a way to understand distress and to build interventions around it. Implementation science exists precisely to close the gap between what's proven to work and what actually gets delivered. Neuroscience pushes the same question further inward, toward the mechanisms of brain, cognition, and behavior that any intervention is ultimately trying to change. Technology and artificial intelligence push it outward, toward how far human capacity can be extended without displacing the judgment and relationships that make care work in the first place — which is why questions of safety, ethics, privacy, and accountability aren't a footnote to that work but a condition of it. And human-centered design is what keeps all of it honest, insisting that interventions and technologies be built for the realities of the people and communities they serve. None of these fields, alone, gets me to systems that are effective, accessible, and built to last. Together, they're the closest thing I've found to an adequate toolkit for the problem.

My interest in organization-building and entrepreneurship comes from the same place. Serving as the Founder of Afrinex, and through my role as Vice-Chairperson of the Board at Growing Up and Liking It (GULI), I continue to explore ways of building youth mental health capacity that go beyond treating illness and toward earlier intervention, peer support, and environments where wellbeing can be supported before crisis. I bring a similar orientation to my current work as a Kectil Leadership & Entrepreneurship Fellow (The Kectil Program, Malmar Knowles Family Foundation) and to my prior role as a Research Volunteer with the FAO, both of which have sharpened how I think about cross-cultural leadership, partnership, and the practical realities of getting good work funded and sustained.

I am building toward a body of work that sits deliberately at that intersection — one that treats people and systems, evidence and technology, as parts of the same question rather than competing priorities.
