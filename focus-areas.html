Interests

My professional interests, in depth

These are the same six interests highlighted on the home page — expanded here into what each one actually means in my work, and why it matters to the bigger question I'm trying to answer.

01

Clinical Psychology

Clinical psychology is where I start: understanding distress well enough to build interventions around it, rather than assuming what should help. That means working from assessment and case conceptualization through to intervention design — grounded in evidence, not trend — and staying close to the realities of the people the work is for.

This is also where my direct-delivery experience lives — facilitating individual and group sessions, monitoring participant progress, and knowing when and how to escalate high-risk cases through the right referral pathway.

02

Scaling Mental Health Interventions

Knowing an intervention works is only the first half of the problem — the second is building the systems that can deliver it to far more people without losing what made it effective in the first place. That's the shift I made over years at the Shamiri Institute: moving from delivering sessions myself to coordinating the projects, teams, and processes that let good programs keep working as they grow.

Scaling well means treating fidelity, training, and monitoring as part of the design from the start, not an afterthought bolted on once a pilot succeeds.

03

Implementation Science

Implementation science exists precisely to close the gap between "the program exists" and "the program works, at scale, in this setting." It's the discipline behind questions like: what does it actually take for a proven intervention to get delivered, by the people who have to deliver it, in the conditions they really work in?

My work with the Nakuru County Department of Mental Health Technical Working Group — work plans, monitoring frameworks, stakeholder coordination — is implementation science applied at a public-sector, systems level rather than a single-program one.

04

Artificial Intelligence in Mental Health

AI pushes the same question outward: how far can human capacity be extended — in prevention, early identification, triage, and referral — without displacing the judgment and relationships that make care work in the first place? I'm interested in AI as a way to extend reach, not replace clinical judgment.

That's why questions of safety, ethics, privacy, and accountability aren't a footnote to this work for me — they're a condition of it. Any tool has to be built and evaluated with those questions in from the start, not patched on afterward.

05

Clinical & Cognitive Neuroscience

Neuroscience pushes the question further inward — toward the mechanisms of brain, cognition, and behavior that any intervention is ultimately trying to change. Understanding those mechanisms is part of what keeps intervention design honest: it's easier to build something that actually changes an outcome when you understand, even partially, what you're trying to change.

This is the layer that keeps me curious about why an intervention works when it does, and not only whether it does.

06

Human-Centered Innovation

Human-centered design is what keeps all of the above honest. It insists that interventions and technologies be built for the realities of the people and communities they're meant to serve — not the conditions a textbook, a grant proposal, or a well-funded pilot site assumes.

My work at Kivuli Wellness Kenya — coordinating outreach, training, and partnerships between schools, healthcare facilities, and local leaders — is where this shows up most concretely: building for the setting you actually have.
