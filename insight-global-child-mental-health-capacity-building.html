Building Local Capacity in Global Mental Health: A Welcome Signal from Kenya

A recent Child Mind Institute post on exploring a Kenya expansion for the SNF Global Center's fellowship program points to a broader, necessary shift in how child mental health capacity gets built worldwide.

Published September 12, 2026 · Read the original

This post was AI-drafted from published reporting, then reviewed and approved by Marvin before publishing. See "Sources & References" below for the original material this commentary is responding to.

I came across a blog post from the Child Mind Institute describing a visit to Kenya by Peter Raucci, who directs Global Fellowships Strategy for the Stavros Niarchos Foundation (SNF) Global Center for Child and Adolescent Mental Health. The piece describes exploring a possible expansion of the Center's work into the region. It's a short, personal account rather than a research paper, but it touches on something those of us working in behavioral health across African contexts think about constantly: how capacity actually gets built, and who gets to build it.

The development itself, as reported, is modest in scope — an exploratory visit tied to a fellowship program that trains and connects professionals working in child and adolescent mental health across countries. What's notable isn't a specific outcome or data point (the summary doesn't offer one, and I won't pretend otherwise), but the model implied: a global center reaching outward to a country like Kenya to ask what expansion could look like, rather than arriving with a fixed template to install.

Why does this matter for those of us in program design and workforce development? Because the history of global mental health initiatives is full of well-funded efforts that didn't survive contact with local realities — different referral systems, different family structures, different ideas about what constitutes distress or care-seeking. Fellowship and exchange models, done well, are one of the more sensible ways to close that gap. They move knowledge and relationships in both directions instead of just exporting a curriculum. That said, a single exploratory visit doesn't tell us much about implementation, sustainability, or fit until there's more to evaluate — and I'd be cautious about reading too much into a preliminary step.

For practitioners and program teams here in Kenya and the wider region, the relevant question isn't whether an international center is interested — interest is easy. It's what capacity-building actually means in practice: Is it training that builds durable local expertise, or a rotating cast of visiting experts? Are local clinicians and researchers shaping the agenda, or just implementing one set elsewhere? Those questions matter more than the announcement of an exploratory visit, and they're the ones I'll be watching for as this initiative, if it moves forward, takes shape.

The honest takeaway is that this is early-stage news, not a finished program with results to assess. What it does offer is a useful prompt for the field: as global mental health organizations look to expand into low- and middle-income countries, the test of a genuine capacity-building effort will be whether local professionals end up with more autonomy and infrastructure five years on — not just more visits.
