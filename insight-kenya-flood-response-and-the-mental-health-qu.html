When the Floods Recede, the Mental Health Work Is Just Beginning

A WHO update on Kenya's flood preparedness is a reminder that health-system readiness has to include psychosocial support, not just physical infrastructure.

Published September 8, 2026 · Read the original

This post was AI-drafted from published reporting, then reviewed and approved by Marvin before publishing. See "Sources & References" below for the original material this commentary is responding to.

WHO AFRO recently highlighted preparedness work ahead of anticipated El Niño-related flooding in Kenya, including the deployment of WHO-trained Rapid Responders following the April flash flood in Mai Mahiu. It's a useful prompt to think about what "health system readiness" actually needs to include when a community is bracing for another disaster.

According to WHO AFRO, Rapid Responders were on the ground evaluating the aftermath of the Mai Mahiu flood and providing essential health support to affected communities — work that typically spans everything from emergency medical care to helping people navigate the practical disruption of losing homes, livelihoods, and routines.

From a systems perspective, disaster response of this kind is a genuine test of referral pathways and care coordination — the same infrastructure that, in calmer periods, is supposed to connect people to routine mental health and psychosocial care. When flooding recurs in a region, as El Niño patterns suggest it may in parts of Kenya, the responders and systems in place during the acute phase often become the same touchpoints people rely on for psychosocial support in the weeks and months after — a connection worth being deliberate about rather than assuming it happens automatically.

Public health emergency responses increasingly build in a psychosocial support component alongside physical health services, precisely because the mental health impact of disasters — displacement, loss, disrupted routines — tends to surface only after the acute emergency has passed, once people are trying to rebuild. Whether this specific WHO Kenya response includes an explicit mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) element isn't detailed in this particular update, but it's the kind of detail worth watching for in follow-up reporting from WHO AFRO.

For practitioners and program teams working in Kenya's health systems, the open question this raises is less "did the emergency response work" and more "what happens to the people it helped once the Rapid Responders move to the next site." That's where longer-term systems and referral pathways — not just emergency response — end up mattering most.
